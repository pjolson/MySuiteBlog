# The Signs a Client Has Outgrown QuickBooks, and What the Move Really Costs

You see it before the client does. The numbers still come out, the close still lands, and nothing is visibly broken. The finance team is simply working harder every month to produce the same result, and the system is the reason.

## Four Signals the Client Is Already Feeling

None of this is news to the client. They have noticed every one of these. Nobody has put them in a single list and named the cause.

**The close creeps past day 10.** What used to be a task has become a project. Two entities and an intercompany elimination mean somebody rebuilds the same consolidation spreadsheet every month, from scratch, under time pressure.

**The real books live outside the accounting system.** Commission calculations, revenue recognition schedules, and deferred revenue balances sit in spreadsheets that one person maintains. QuickBooks holds the output. The work happens somewhere else.

**One person owns the close.** Month-end runs on knowledge that was never written down. That person takes a week off, close slips, and everyone treats the slip as unavoidable. Ask who the backup is and the honest answer is that there is not one.

**Revenue is re-keyed by hand.** Orders leave the CRM or the operations system as an export, and somebody pastes them into QuickBooks. When volume doubles, so does the typing. The integration is a person.

## What the Proposal Does Not Price

The partner proposal covers two things: the software subscription and the implementation build. Both are priced carefully. Neither is where the project goes wrong.

What the proposal does not price is the work that stays on the client's side of the table. Testing scripts have to be written and executed by people who already have full-time jobs. Data migration forces decisions nobody outside the company can make: which fields come across, how much history, which entities, what gets left behind. User acceptance testing lands in the same two weeks as month-end, every time. And the finance team has to keep running the old system while building the new one, because the business does not pause for a go-live.

This is where projects stall for six months. Not because the software failed, and not because the partner was bad at the job. The partner delivered what the statement of work described. The work on the other side of that statement had no owner.

I have sat in that seat. A healthcare services firm I worked for went live on NetSuite the year I joined, and inside a year I owned the program: a consultant handled the scripting, and the decisions the partner could not make for us were mine.

## The Line Nobody Budgets

Three costs sit behind this decision. Everyone quotes the first. Everyone expects the second. Almost nobody counts the third.

The subscription is a number on a page. The implementation partner is a number on a proposal. The internal time is three people giving twenty percent of their week for six months, which is roughly a full-time salary the company spends without ever putting it in a budget.

That changes the question. It stops being whether NetSuite is worth ten thousand dollars a month, and becomes whether the company has the people to land it, and which of them is accountable for that work.

## Who Owns the Client's Side

The partner is gone at go-live. The controller already has a job.

It is someone who has run NetSuite from inside a company rather than only from across the table. Someone who has closed a month in the finance seat and knows which corners cost you in March. Someone whose only job during the project is the company's half of it.

Software decisions do not fail on software. They fail on whether the client's half of the work had a name attached to it.
